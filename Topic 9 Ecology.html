🏭 TOPIC 9: Occupational Health and Safety

Workplace Hazards, Occupational Pathology, Risk Evaluation & Hierarchy of Controls

1. What is Occupational Health and Safety (OHS)?

⚙️ Industrial Worker Scenario: A factory technician operates high-speed unguarded stamping machinery, breathes solvent fumes daily, labors under unventilated furnace heat, and faces chronic shift-work stress. All these environmental stressors directly affect his health, well-being, and longevity.

Occupational Health and Safety (OHS) is the multi-disciplinary science and art devoted to the anticipation, recognition, evaluation, and control of environmental factors or stresses arising in or from the workplace that may cause sickness, impaired health and well-being, or significant discomfort among workers or citizens of the surrounding community.

Occupational Safety vs. Occupational Health

DomainPrimary FocusCharacteristic Examples
Occupational Safety Preventing immediate, acute workplace accidents and mechanical injuries. Falls from scaffolds, lacerations from rotating blades, electrocution, burns from molten metal.
Occupational Health Preserving overall physiological and mental well-being; preventing acute and chronic occupational diseases. Pneumoconiosis from silica dust, noise-induced hearing loss, benzene-induced leukemia, occupational stress.
🧠 Memory Trick:
• Safety = Prevent immediate accidents and traumatic injuries.
• Health = Prevent systemic illnesses and preserve long-term physical/mental well-being.

2. Scope of OHS & Hazard Recognition

The operational scope of OHS comprises three core progressive stages:

  • 1. Recognition & Anticipation: Identifying existing or potential workplace hazards before harm manifests.
  • 2. Evaluation: Quantitative monitoring, air sampling, and risk analysis to evaluate exposure severity.
  • 3. Corrective Action: Designing and implementing engineering, administrative, and personal safeguards.

The Walk-Through Survey:

A systematic on-site walkthrough inspection designed to identify workplace hazards. Key investigative questions:

  • What final commodities and byproducts are being manufactured?
  • What raw chemical materials, heavy metals, or biological agents are used?
  • What machines, mechanical tools, and physical energy sources are involved?
  • What specific operating procedures and shifts are followed by workers?
  • Is general dilution and local exhaust ventilation adequate?
  • Are emergency eyewash stations, safety showers, and appropriate respirators functional?
  • Are standard safety operating procedures consistently practiced?

4. Physical Hazards Detailed

A. Extreme Temperature (Heat Stress Spectrum)

When exposed to severe ambient heat, the body attempts thermoregulation by increasing cardiac output, dilating cutaneous capillaries, and producing sweat. Failure leads to four heat syndromes:

ConditionPathophysiology & PresentationClinical Distinctions
Heat Stress The overall physiological load; the body struggles to maintain normal core temperature. Early discomfort and fatigue.
Heat Cramps Painful skeletal muscle spasms following heavy physical exertion in heat. Caused by profuse sweating and loss of sodium chloride and fluids.
Heat Exhaustion Systemic volume depletion secondary to prolonged sweating without adequate fluid/salt replacement. Manifests with weakness, vertigo, headache, and profuse diaphoresis.
Heat Stroke A catastrophic medical emergency; core temperature rises (>104°F/40°C) with thermoregulatory failure. Complete cessation of sweating, hot dry skin, delirium, and coma.

B. Ionizing vs. Non-Ionizing Radiation

  • Ionizing Radiation: High-energy radiation with sufficient energy to strip orbital electrons from atoms, creating mutagenic ions that damage DNA.
    • Forms: X-rays, Gamma rays, Alpha particles, Beta particles, Neutrons, Protons.
    • Exposure Routes: External (source outside body) vs. Internal (inhalation, ingestion, or contaminated wounds).
  • Non-Ionizing Radiation: Lower-energy electromagnetic waves incapable of ionizing biological molecules.
    • Sources: Radio broadcast towers, microwave ovens, power transmission lines, lasers, infrared heaters.

C. Excessive Acoustic Noise

  • Auditory Impacts: Temporary Threshold Shift (TTS) and irreversible permanent sensorineural hearing loss.
  • Non-Auditory Impacts: Chronic fatigue, impaired communication, elevated blood pressure, autonomic nervous system stress, and reduced workplace vigilance.

5. Mechanical & Chemical Hazards

Mechanical Hazards

Result from direct physical contact with machinery, moving components, and tools:

  • Crush injuries, limb amputations, and entanglement of loose clothing in drive belts.
  • Repetitive physical pressure from manual tools causing tenosynovitis of the hands, prepatellar bursitis (beat knee), and olecranon bursitis (beat elbow).

Chemical Hazards

Chemical substances occurring as dusts, mists, fumes, gases, and vapors. The severity of toxic injury depends on chemical potency, concentration, and exposure duration.

Three Primary Routes of Occupational Chemical Exposure:

1. Inhalation: The fastest, most critical occupational route
2. Skin Absorption: Dermal penetration of solvents and pesticides
3. Ingestion: Accidental oral ingestion via contaminated hands, food, or smoking

6. Biological, Ergonomic & Psychosocial Hazards

Biological Hazards

Living infectious pathogens (viruses, bacteria, fungi) and bio-aerosols. Clinical example: Accidental percutaneous puncture of a healthcare worker's finger with a hollow-bore needle contaminated with Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, or HIV.

Ergonomic Hazards

Derived from Greek Ergon (work) and Nomos (laws). The scientific discipline of fitting the workplace, machines, and tools to the worker's physical body—not forcing the human body to adapt to poor design.

  • Poor ergonomics causes: Chronic lumbosacral strain, carpal tunnel syndrome, cervical tension, and musculoskeletal fatigue.

Psychosocial Hazards

Organizational factors inducing mental and emotional strain: unmanageable work pace, lack of operational control, shift work, and hostile workplace relationships leading to burnout, depression, and cardiovascular hypertension.

8. Control of Workplace Hazards (Hierarchy of Controls)

Occupational hygiene prioritizes eliminating or controlling the hazard at its physical source before relying on worker-level protection:

A. Substitution (Source Elimination)

Replacing a hazardous material or machine with an inherently safer alternative. Examples: Substituting lead pigments with non-toxic titanium dioxide; replacing lead solder with tin solder; substituting toxic benzene with toluene.

B. Isolation

Placing a physical barrier between the dangerous process and workers, or operating high-radiation/high-noise operations inside acoustic/lead-lined containment booths.

C. Engineering Controls

Physical plant modifications such as automated machine guards, interlocks, and local exhaust ventilation capturing fumes directly at the generation point.

D. Industrial Ventilation

  • Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV): Captures air contaminants directly at the point of origin before they disperse into breathing zones.
  • Dilution Ventilation: Introduces large volumes of fresh outdoor air to dilute contaminant concentrations.

E. Administrative Controls

Modifying work schedules and organizational behaviors: job rotation to limit individual exposure time, mandatory rest breaks in heat, employee training, and scheduled preventative maintenance.

F. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

The last line of defense, employed when engineering controls cannot completely eliminate exposure:

1. Respiratory: Half/full-face elastomeric respirators & particulate filters
2. Eye & Face: Chemical splash goggles and face shields
3. Dermal: Impermeable chemical suits, aprons, and nitrile gloves
4. Acoustic: Acoustic earplugs and noise-attenuating earmuffs
⚠️ Exam Golden Rule (Hierarchy of Control): Eliminating or substituting a hazard (e.g., using water-based paint instead of toxic solvent paint) is vastly superior to providing a respirator, because PPE relies entirely on human compliance and fit.
