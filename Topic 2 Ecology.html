🌍 TOPIC 2: Human Ecology & Human–Environment Interaction

Core Components, Ecological Triangle, and Environmental Approaches

In Topic 1, we learned that ecology studies the relationship between living organisms and their environment. Now we will focus on one special relationship:

Core Question: How do humans affect their environment, and how does the environment affect human life and health?

This topic is important in public health because many diseases and health problems are connected to the way people live, behave and interact with their surroundings.

1. What is Human Ecology?

According to your lecture, human ecology focuses on the relationship between humans and the natural, social and built environment.

🏙️ Let's make it simple — Imagine a student living in Jimma:
  • Natural environment: rainfall, temperature, rivers, soil and vegetation.
  • Social environment: family, friends, culture, beliefs and community relationships.
  • Built environment: houses, roads, schools, hospitals and markets.

All these things interact with the student's life and health.

For example: Poor housing may expose someone to cold, dampness and overcrowding. These conditions can increase health risks.

📌 Remember: Human ecology is not just about humans living in nature. It is about the two-way relationship between humans and their surroundings.

2. Core Components of Human Ecology

Your lecture identifies four core components:

1. Human Population Dynamics

Characteristics of the human population, such as age, gender and genetics.

Human population: Demographics, age, gender, and genetics

2. Human Adaptation and Interaction

How humans adjust to and interact with their environment through cultural practices and technological innovations.

Adaptation through culture and technology

3. Social and Organizational Systems

Economic frameworks, political structures and urban planning.

Societal infrastructure, economics, and organization

4. Environment

Natural landscapes and the built environment in which humans live.

Natural and built surroundings
🏛️ Simple Analogy: Think of a university campus. Students are the population; their habits are behavior; the campus surroundings are the habitat; and university rules and organization are part of the social system.

3. The Triangle of Human Ecology

🔥 Very Important for Exams: Your lecture identifies three main categories of factors that affect human health: Population, Behavior, and Habitat.
The human ecology triangle: Population, Behavior, and Habitat

Think of health as a three-legged stool. If one leg becomes unstable, the whole stool may become unstable.

A. Population

This refers to characteristics of people, including:

  • Genetics: inherited characteristics and genetic susceptibility.
  • Age: some diseases are more common in particular age groups.
  • Gender: some health risks differ between males and females.

Example: Older adults have a higher risk of stroke than younger adults. Your lecture specifically discusses age-related stroke risk.

⚠️ Exam trick: Age and genetic predisposition belong to population, not behavior.

B. Behavior

Behavior refers to human actions, beliefs and practices that influence health. Examples:

  • Smoking
  • Handwashing
  • Food choices
  • Sexual practices
  • Use of health services

Example: Two people live in the same village and drink water from the same source. One consistently washes their hands before eating, while the other does not. Their behaviors may influence their risk of diarrheal disease.

⚠️ Exam trick: Ask yourself, "What does the person do or practice?" If the question focuses on actions or habits, think behavior.

C. Habitat

Habitat refers to the surroundings in which people live, including the social and built environment. Examples:

  • Housing conditions
  • Roads and transport
  • Water availability
  • Sanitation facilities
  • Social conditions surrounding people

Example: A person living in a crowded house with poor ventilation may face a greater risk of respiratory infection.

⚠️ Exam trick: Ask, "Where and under what surroundings does the person live?" That points to habitat.

Let's Distinguish Them:

SituationTriangle Component
A 70-year-old personPopulation
A person who smokesBehavior
A family living in an overcrowded houseHabitat
Genetic susceptibility to cancerPopulation
Poor handwashing practiceBehavior
Poor sanitation in a communityHabitat

Important: These three factors interact. A person's age, behavior and living conditions can combine to influence their health.

4. Human–Environment Interaction Across Eras

The relationship between humans and the environment changes over time. Compare two periods:

Stone Age:

  • Hunting and gathering.
  • Simple tools.
  • Strong dependence on natural resources.
Stone Age: High dependency on pristine natural resources

Modern Age:

  • Industrialization.
  • Advanced technology.
  • Urbanization.
  • Greater human influence on nature.
Modern Era: Industrial expansion and environmental modification

Ecosystem Interactions Generate Three Important Outcomes:

  1. Trophic Stages: Feeding relationships represented by food chains and food webs.
    Example: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle.
    Food chains, food webs, and trophic levels
  2. Biodiversity: The variety of living organisms in an area. For example, a forest may contain trees, birds, insects, fungi and mammals.
  3. Material Cycles and Energy Flow: Materials such as water, carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus move through ecosystems in cycles. Energy, however, flows through food chains and some is lost as heat at each trophic level.
    Cyclic matter and unidirectional energy dissipation
📌 Exam Distinction:
• Matter is recycled.
• Energy flows through the ecosystem and is gradually dissipated as heat.

5. Five Approaches to Human–Environment Interaction

Your lecture describes five approaches. This is an important section because exam questions may give you a situation and ask you to identify the approach.

A. Deterministic Approach (Environment Controls Humans)

The basic principle is "Earth made man." It emphasizes that the natural environment strongly controls human activities and human life.

❄️ Analogy: Imagine living in a very cold region. The climate influences the clothes you wear, the type of house you build and some of your daily activities. The environment is considered dominant, while humans are subordinate to it.

Exam clue: Environment determines or controls human activities.

B. Teleological Approach (Nature Exists for a Purpose)

This approach views things as existing or happening to serve a purpose or final goal. Your lecture links this approach to the belief that humans are superior to nature and other creatures, and that nature exists for human use and enjoyment.

🌲 Analogy: Someone believes that forests exist mainly for humans to cut trees, build houses and obtain resources.

Potential consequence: Uncontrolled exploitation of natural resources.
Exam clue: Human superiority and nature existing for human use.

C. Possibilistic Approach (Humans Can Influence Nature, but Cannot Fully Control It)

This approach emerged as a criticism of environmental determinism and the excessive human-control idea associated with teleological thinking. It recognizes two things:

  • The physical environment influences human activities.
  • Humans can also influence and modify their environment.

But humans cannot completely tame nature.

🌧️ Analogy: Humans cannot stop rainfall, but they can construct dams, irrigation systems and drainage channels to respond to it.

Exam clue: Human adjustment, response and possibilities within environmental limits.

D. Economic Deterministic Approach (Economic Growth is Prioritized)

This approach emphasizes economic and industrial expansion through modern technology. It gives greater importance to economic growth, political stability and social stability than to the associated harmful effects on environmental quality.

🏭 Analogy: A government allows factories to expand rapidly because they create employment and income, even though they pollute rivers and air.

Possible consequence: Overexploitation of natural resources and environmental deterioration.
Exam clue: Economic growth is prioritized despite environmental damage.

E. Ecological Approach (Humans and Nature Interact Mutually)

This approach is based on the ecological principle that humans and other organisms interact with one another and with their environment. Humans are considered an integral part of nature, not separate from it.

👨‍🌾 Analogy: A farmer depends on soil, water, sunlight and insects. The farmer also affects the environment through farming practices. Both influence each other.

Exam clue: Mutual interaction, interdependence and reciprocal relationship.

Compare All Five Approaches:

ApproachMain Idea
DeterministicEnvironment controls humans
TeleologicalNature exists for human purpose/use
PossibilisticHumans adjust to and influence nature
Economic deterministicEconomic growth is prioritized
EcologicalHumans and nature mutually interact
🧠 Memory Trick:
• Deterministic = Environment dominates.
• Teleological = Purpose / Human superiority.
• Possibilistic = Possibilities within limits.
• Economic deterministic = Economy first.
• Ecological = Exchange and mutual interaction.

6. When Human–Environment Interaction Becomes Harmful

Human activities can disturb the natural environment, especially when environmental changes exceed the ecosystem's ability to regulate or recover from them. Examples include:

  • Deforestation
  • Unplanned urbanization
  • Excessive exploitation of natural resources
  • Industrial pollution
  • Poor waste disposal
  • Destruction of natural habitats

These changes can affect human health and ecological balance.

🦟 Public Health Example: Cutting down forests and changing land use can alter mosquito habitats. This may change the risk of mosquito-borne diseases. The key idea is that human actions produce environmental changes that eventually circle back to affect human health.

7. Exam Application: Identify the Triangle and Approach

Scenario: Imagine a village where malaria cases increase after a new irrigation project.

Consider the following:

  • The construction of irrigation channels changes the environment.
  • Standing water provides breeding sites for mosquitoes.
  • People's activities and exposure influence their risk.
  • The age and other characteristics of individuals may affect their vulnerability.
💡 Clinical Takeaway: This example connects human–environment interaction with population, behavior and habitat. Human ecology helps us understand why health problems occur in particular populations and environments, rather than looking only at the individual patient.

8. Quick Revision — Key Takeaways

  • Human ecology studies relationships between humans and natural, social and built environments.
  • Core components include population dynamics, human adaptation and interaction, social and organizational systems, and environment.
  • The triangle of human ecology is population, behavior and habitat.
  • Population includes age, gender and genetic characteristics.
  • Behavior includes actions, beliefs and practices.
  • Habitat includes social and built surroundings.
  • Human–environment interaction generates trophic relationships, biodiversity, material cycles and energy flow.
  • Five approaches: deterministic, teleological, possibilistic, economic deterministic and ecological.
