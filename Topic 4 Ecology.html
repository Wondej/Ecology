🌍 TOPIC 4: Ecology of Health and Disease

Dynamic Equilibrium, Maladaptation, Epidemiological Triangle & Natural History of Disease

🔥 The Fire Analogy: Think of disease like a fire: a fire needs fuel, oxygen, and suitable conditions. Similarly, disease occurrence depends on the interaction of an infectious agent, a host, and the environment.

This topic explains why diseases occur, how environmental changes can create disease risks, and how we can predict and control them.

1. Historical Theories of Disease

Your PDF introduces two important theories:

A. Spontaneous Generation Theory

This was the belief that life and disease could arise directly from non-living matter.

  • Simple example: People once believed that maggots appeared spontaneously from rotting meat rather than developing from fly eggs.
  • The important idea: People believed living things could emerge from non-living materials without coming from existing life.

B. Germ Theory

The view that disease is the product of a particular pathogen infecting a particular patient.

  • Example: Tuberculosis occurs when a person becomes infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
  • Pathogen: The disease-causing microorganism.
  • Patient / Host: The person who can become infected.
  • Infection: Invasion and multiplication of the microorganism in the body.
📌 Exam Distinction:
• Spontaneous generation: An obsolete belief about the origin of life and disease from non-living matter.
• Germ theory: Links infectious disease to specific biological pathogens.

2. Disease Ecology & Dynamic Equilibrium

Disease ecology studies how humans, society, and the physical and biological environment interact in relation to disease.

Your PDF emphasizes that these components exist in dynamic equilibrium.

⚖️ What does dynamic equilibrium mean?
Imagine a community where:
  • People live in a certain environment.
  • Their activities affect the environment.
  • Animals, insects, and microorganisms also live there.
  • Natural and social conditions influence everyone's health.
These components continuously affect one another. The balance is not fixed; it is dynamic and changes over time.
Interplay of climate, ecology, and vector-borne diseases

What Happens When Equilibrium is Disturbed?

Major changes such as:

  • Land-use changes
  • Migration
  • Population pressure
  • Other environmental stressors

can disturb the human–environment relationship. This may cause maladaptation, which can manifest as the emergence or spread of infectious diseases.

🌊 Analogy: Imagine a river valley. If people build a dam and create stagnant-water habitats, mosquito and snail populations increase. More vectors mean a greater risk of vector-borne illnesses.

3. Maladaptation: When Environmental Change Increases Disease

Maladaptation in this context means a harmful mismatch or disturbance in the relationship between humans and their environment, which creates or amplifies disease risks.

Your PDF highlights five classic global examples:

4. Vector-Borne Disease: Epidemic vs. Non-Epidemic Conditions

A vector is a living organism that transmits an infectious agent from one host to another (e.g., mosquitoes, ticks, snails).

ConditionSpatial/Ecological RelationshipDisease Transmission Risk
Non-epidemic Environment No overlap between vector habitats and human populations Low / Minimal risk of transmission
Epidemic Environment Active spatial & temporal overlap between vectors and humans High risk; promotes outbreaks and transmission
Barrier protection preventing human-vector contact
Vector proliferation creating human-vector overlap and epidemic risk

Measuring Seasonal Epidemics:

Seasonal epidemics can be assessed by tracking changes in human-vector overlap across two dimensions:

  • Time: When the overlap occurs (e.g., peak rainy season months).
  • Space: Where the overlap occurs (e.g., lowlands near standing water pools).

Example: During rainy seasons, mosquito breeding sites proliferate. If people are in close contact with infected mosquitoes, malaria transmission surges.

5. The Agent–Host–Environment Model (Epidemiological Triangle)

Disease results from the continuous, complex interaction of three fundamental elements:

A. Agent (The "What")

The factor that is necessary to cause disease.

  • Biological: Bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungi.
  • Chemical: Toxic pollutants, poisons, heavy metals.
  • Physical: Excessive heat, radiation, mechanical trauma.

B. Host (The "Who")

The person or organism capable of acquiring or developing the disease. Host factors include:

  • Age and sex
  • Genetic characteristics
  • Nutritional status
  • Immunological competence
  • Pre-existing health conditions
  • Personal habits and behaviors

C. Environment (The "Where")

External physical, biological, and socioeconomic conditions influencing both agent and host:

  • Temperature, humidity, and rainfall
  • Housing quality and sanitation facilities
  • Safe water availability
  • Population density and crowding
  • Socioeconomic status and health services

Applying the Triangle: Malaria

Triangle ElementSpecific Factor in Malaria
AgentPlasmodium parasite
HostHuman (immunity, age, bednet usage)
EnvironmentRainfall, temperature supporting Anopheles mosquitoes & breeding sites
⚖️ How Does Imbalance Cause Disease?
The agent and host are in a constant struggle for survival. When the environment maintains equilibrium between them, disease is contained. But when an environmental disturbance shifts the balance in favor of the agent (or weakens the host), disease outbreaks occur.

6. Disease as Maladjustment to the Environment

The PDF gives an overarching ecological definition:

📖 Key Concept: Disease is "maladjustment of the humans to the environment".

In simple language, illness emerges when the harmonious relationship between a person and their surrounding environment is disrupted.

Example: Poor community sanitation contaminates drinking water with human feces. A person drinks the water and develops severe diarrhea:

  • Agent: Enteric pathogen (e.g., Vibrio cholerae, E. coli).
  • Host: Susceptible person consuming the water.
  • Environment: Unsafe water source, absent latrines, poor hygiene.

This demonstrates why environmental health measures (sanitation and clean water) are fundamental to disease prevention.

7. Disease Distribution & Natural History of Disease

Disease Distribution

Defined as the pattern and frequency of health events across a population, analyzed according to three core epidemiological dimensions:

  • Time (When?): In which month, season, or year does the disease peak?
  • Place (Where?): In which village, town, geographic region, or topography?
  • Person (Who?): Which age bracket, sex, occupation, or socioeconomic group?
🧠 Memory Trick: T–P–P = Time, Place, Person.

Application: If malaria cases spike during the rainy season in a specific district, especially among children under five:
  • Time: Rainy season
  • Place: That specific district
  • Person: Children under 5 years of age

Natural History of Disease

Your PDF also equates disease ecology with understanding the natural history of disease. It enables epidemiologists and public health leaders to:

  • Predict when a disease outbreak is likely to happen.
  • Predict where it will manifest spatially.
  • Design targeted interventions before transmission accelerates.

Example: Knowing that malaria increases during specific post-rainy months in a district allows public health teams to pre-position rapid diagnostic tests, artemisinin combination therapy (ACT), and insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs) well in advance.
