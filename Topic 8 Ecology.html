🦟 TOPIC 8: Vector Control and Food Hygiene

Arthropod Vectors, Biological Transmission, Foodborne Pathology & Safe Handling

PART 1: Vector Control

1. What is a Vector?

A vector is an organism, commonly an insect (arthropod) or rodent, that transmits infectious disease-causing agents to humans.

  • Mosquitoes: Transmit malaria, dengue, yellow fever, and Zika.
  • Houseflies: Mechanically carry enteric pathogens directly to food.
  • Fleas: Transmit plague (Yersinia pestis).
  • Sandflies: Transmit leishmaniasis.
  • Ticks: Transmit Lyme disease and viral hemorrhagic fevers.
Mosquito lifecycle: Egg, larva, pupa, adult
Housefly: Mechanical carrier of pathogens
Sandfly: Vector of leishmaniasis
Ticks: Vectors of Lyme disease and rickettsia

2. Classification of Vectors

A. Mechanical Vectors

Transmit pathogens passively by carrying them externally on their feet, mouthparts, body hairs, or gastrointestinal tract without biological multiplication or structural development.

Musca domestica: Passive mechanical transfer from feces to food

B. Biological Vectors

The disease-causing pathogen undergoes essential physiological development, multiplication, or sexual cycles inside the vector's body prior to transmission.

Plasmodium sporogony inside Anopheles mosquito
FeatureMechanical VectorBiological Vector
Mode of TransportPassive surface carriageActive internal development / multiplication
Pathogen DevelopmentNone requiredEssential biological cycle occurs inside vector
Classic ExamplesHouseflies, CockroachesMosquitoes (Malaria), Ticks (Lyme), Sandflies
🚗 Analogy: A mechanical vector is like a dirty vehicle carrying dust from one place to another. A biological vector acts as a biological incubator where the pathogen matures and multiplies.

3. Four Modes of Biological Transmission (High Yield)

Transmission TypeDevelopmental Changes?Pathogen Multiplication?Exam Examples
1. Propagative No (Morphology remains same) Yes (Multiplies in number) Yellow fever virus, Louse-borne relapsing fever
2. Cyclo-developmental Yes (Undergoes developmental stages) No (No numerical multiplication) Lymphatic filariasis (Wuchereria bancrofti microfilariae)
3. Transovarian (Hereditary) Passed into vector ovarian eggs Maintains infection across generations Tick-borne relapsing fever
4. Cyclo-propagative Yes (Cyclic morphological transitions) Yes (Undergoes massive multiplication) Malaria (Plasmodium species in Anopheles)
🧠 Mnemonic:
• Propagative = Propagation only (Multiply alone).
• Cyclo-developmental = Developmental cycle only (Transform alone).
• Transovarian = Ovaries/Eggs to next generation.
• Cyclo-propagative = Both transform AND multiply!

5. Methods of Vector Control

Your PDF presents six integrated approaches:

1. Personal Protection

Wearing protective long-sleeved clothing, window screening, sleeping under Insecticide-Treated Nets (ITNs/LLINs), and topical repellents (DEET).

Distribution and utilization of long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs)

2. Chemical Control

Application of synthetic insecticides (Indoor Residual Spraying - IRS) and larvicides (e.g., malathion, propoxur). Historical note: PDF mentions historical organochlorines (DDT, lindane, aldrin) strictly for taxonomic reference.

Indoor Residual Spraying (IRS) and chemical vector control

3. Environmental Manipulation (Source Reduction)

Eliminating breeding sites by draining standing stagnant water, ditching, clearing discarded tires, and covering domestic water storage jars.

Eliminating stagnant larval breeding containers around homes

4. Biological Control

Introducing natural predators or pathogens targeting larval stages:

  • Gambusia affinis (larvivorous "mosquito fish") stocked in standing pools.
  • Bacillus thuringiensis israelensis (Bti) bacterial bio-larvicide.
  • Dragonfly nymphs preying on larvae.
Gambusia affinis: Larvivorous fish consuming mosquito larvae

5. Genetic Control

Sterilizing male insects using gamma radiation or chemosterilants (Sterile Insect Technique) or releasing genetically modified strains to crash vector reproductive viability.

Release of sterile or genetically modified mosquitoes to suppress wild populations

6. Rodents and Public Health

Rats and mice contaminate domestic environments and transmit lethal zoonoses:

🐀 Hantavirus Pulmonary Syndrome (HPS): Contracted by inhaling aerosolized dust particles contaminated with urine, dried droppings, or saliva from infected rodents. Can cause acute, rapidly fatal respiratory failure.

PART 2: Food Hygiene

7. What is Food Hygiene?

According to your PDF, food hygiene encompasses all conditions and measures necessary to ensure the safety, wholesomeness, and suitability of food at every stage of its growth, production, distribution, preparation, and final consumption (farm to fork).

Sanitary food handling, preparation, and glove utilization

Points of Potential Contamination:

  • Production: Agricultural runoff, raw sewage irrigation, infected livestock.
  • Transportation: Unrefrigerated dirty transport trucks.
  • Preparation: Infected food handlers, unwashed hands, contaminated cutting boards.
  • Storage: Temperature danger zone, fly and rodent infestations.
  • Consumption: Undercooked meats, unwashed raw produce.

8. Foodborne Diseases Classification

Foodborne illnesses result from ingesting food containing pathogenic biological, chemical, or physical agents. Your PDF makes a critical tripartite distinction:

Clinical CategoryPathophysiological MechanismClassic Example
A. Foodborne Infection Ingestion of food containing viable, living pathogens that colonize, invade, and multiply within the host intestine. Salmonella species, Campylobacter jejuni
B. Foodborne Intoxication Ingestion of food containing pre-formed exotoxins produced by microorganisms multiplying in the food prior to consumption. Clostridium botulinum (Botulism), Staphylococcus aureus enterotoxin
C. Toxicoinfection (Toxin-Mediated) Ingestion of large numbers of viable bacteria that subsequently sporulate or release enterotoxins in vivo inside the digestive tract. Clostridium perfringens, Bacillus cereus

High-Risk Vulnerable Populations:

  • Infants and toddlers
  • Elderly persons (>65 years)
  • Immunocompromised patients (cancer, organ transplant, HIV/AIDS)
  • Pregnant women (risk of fetal loss via Listeria or Toxoplasma)
  • Malnourished, nutrient-deprived individuals

10. The 8 Core Principles of Food Hygiene

1. Maintain Personal Hygiene: Thorough handwashing with soap prior to food handling
2. Protect Food from Contamination: Mesh coverings shielding meals from fly contact
3. Keep Equipment Clean: Sterilizing knives, boards, and surfaces after each food item
4. Cook Food Thoroughly: Reaching adequate core temperature to kill vegetative pathogens
5. Keep Hot Food Hot: Consume meals immediately while hot; avoid lukewarm storage
6. Store Food Safely: Rapid cooling and refrigeration within 90 minutes; temp below 5°C
7. Separate Raw and Cooked Food: Prevent transfer of pathogens from raw meat to ready-to-eat food
8. Choose and Wash Food Carefully: Wash fruits and vegetables in potable water

11. Cross-Contamination (Crucial Exam Concept)

Cross-contamination: Pathogen transfer via shared cutting boards
🔪 Classic Clinical Scenario:
• You cut raw chicken contaminated with Salmonella on a wooden chopping board.
• Without washing the board and knife, you slice raw lettuce and tomatoes for salad.
• The live bacteria transfer from the chicken juices onto the salad vegetables, which are eaten raw without cooking.
• Result: Severe salmonellosis food poisoning!
⚠️ Exam Trap: Thoroughly washing salad vegetables will NOT protect consumers if they are subsequently cut on a knife or board previously contaminated with raw meat fluids.
