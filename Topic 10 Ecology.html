🛡️ TOPIC 10: Infection Prevention and Control (IPC)

Nosocomial Infections, Chain of Transmission, Standard Precautions & Isolation Protocols

1. What is Infection?

According to your lecture PDF, infection is the invasion and active multiplication of microorganisms such as:

  • Bacteria
  • Viruses
  • Fungi
  • Parasites

inside the tissues of a host, which can disrupt normal physiological body functions and precipitate clinical disease.

🏠 House Intruder Analogy: Imagine your body is a secure home. Microorganisms are uninvited intruders that enter, replicate without permission, and damage household furnishings. If they disrupt domestic routines, an infection is established. Example: Pathogenic bacteria entering a cutaneous laceration and multiplying to cause an acute, purulent wound infection.

2. Infection Prevention and Control & Nosocomial Infections

Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) is a systematic, evidence-based approach designed to prevent the transmission of infections in healthcare facilities, long-term care homes, and community settings.

⏱️ Nosocomial Infections (Healthcare-Associated Infections - HAIs):
Defined as infections that arise more than 48 hours after admission to a healthcare facility, which were neither present nor incubating at the time of patient admission.

Major Clinical Manifestations of HAIs:

  • Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTI)
  • Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP / Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia)
  • Surgical Site Infections (SSI) in post-operative incisions
  • Catheter-Related Bloodstream Infections (CRBSI / Bacteremia)
  • Gastrointestinal infections (e.g., healthcare-associated Clostridioides difficile)
  • Cutaneous and subcutaneous soft-tissue infections
🏥 Clinical Vignette: A patient is hospitalized for surgical fixation of a closed tibial fracture. On day 4 (well beyond 48 hours post-admission), he develops dysuria, fever, and leukocytosis, yielding Pseudomonas in urine cultures. This represents a classic nosocomial infection.

3. Reservoirs of Nosocomial Infections

A reservoir is any person, animal, arthropod, plant, soil, or substance (or combination of these) in which an infectious agent normally lives and multiplies.

Reservoir CategoryPhysical NatureHospital Examples
Animate Sources Living biological entities harboring pathogens Infected patients, asymptomatic colonized medical personnel, visitors, insects
Inanimate Sources (Fomites) Non-living physical substrates and environmental media Contaminated surgical instruments, bedside rails, IV drip stands, sinks, tap water, medications, dressings
Animate sources: Healthcare workers and patients
Inanimate sources: Wards, clinical surfaces, and medical equipment

4. Portals of Entry/Exit & Modes of Transmission

Portals of Entry and Exit:

  • Broken skin and intact mucous membranes
  • Respiratory tract (inhalation/exhalation)
  • Urinary tract (catheters, instrumentation)
  • Gastrointestinal tract (ingestion/fecal excretion)
  • Reproductive tract (urogenital secretions)
  • Bloodstream (parenteral puncture, vascular cannulas)

Five Major Modes of Disease Transmission:

ModeMechanismClinical Example
Direct Contact Direct physical body surface-to-body surface transfer Skin-to-skin transfer during patient repositioning or bathing
Indirect Contact Transfer via an intermediate contaminated inanimate object (fomite) Unwashed clinician hands transferring MRSA between consecutive patients
Droplet Large respiratory droplets (>5 µm) projected short distances (≤1 meter) Meningococcus or influenza expelled during coughing or sneezing
Airborne Tiny droplet nuclei (≤5 µm) remaining suspended in air currents for hours Inhaling aerosolized Mycobacterium tuberculosis or measles
Vehicles & Vectors Contaminated bulk media (water, food, drugs) or living vectors Contaminated multi-dose medication vials or hospital insects
🔗 The IPC Goal: To intentionally break the chain of infection by blocking transmission routes between reservoirs and vulnerable host portals.

5. Standard Precautions: The Universal Baseline

⭐ The Universal Golden Rule: Standard Precautions represent the minimum infection prevention practices that must be applied to ALL patients at all times, in all healthcare settings, regardless of suspected or confirmed infection status.

Core Components of Standard Precautions:

  • Hand hygiene (the single most effective measure)
  • Personal protective equipment (PPE) selected according to risk assessment
  • Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette
  • Safe injection practices (single-use disposables; zero recapping)
  • Safe cleaning and reprocessing of equipment and environmental surfaces

WHO 5 Moments of Hand Hygiene:

The WHO 5 Moments: 1. Before patient contact, 2. Before clean procedure, 3. After body fluid risk, 4. After patient contact, 5. After touching surroundings
  1. Before touching a patient (protects patient from germs on clinician hands)
  2. Before a clean or aseptic procedure (protects patient from inward pathogen entry)
  3. After body fluid exposure risk (protects clinician and environment)
  4. After touching a patient (protects clinician and healthcare setting)
  5. After touching patient surroundings (protects against contaminated fomites)

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):

Medical gloves: Barrier for blood and body fluids
Particulate respirators (N95 / FFP2) for airborne isolation
Face shields & goggles: Mucosal splash protection
Protective fluid-resistant isolation gowns
⚠️ Vital Exam Rule: Wearing gloves does NOT replace hand hygiene! Hands must be sanitized immediately before donning and immediately after doffing gloves.

6. Transmission-Based Precautions (Second Tier)

Applied in addition to Standard Precautions for patients suspected or confirmed to be infected with highly transmissible pathogens:

A. Contact Precautions

Contact precautions: Gloves and disposable gown upon room entry
  • Indications: Colonization or infection with multidrug-resistant bacteria (e.g., MRSA, VRE), draining abscesses, uncontrolled wound exudate, norovirus, C. diff.
  • Required PPE & Protocol: Single room (or cohorting); wear clean non-sterile gloves and gown upon entering; dedicated patient-care equipment (e.g., stethoscope).

B. Droplet Precautions

Droplet precautions: Surgical mask required within close proximity
  • Indications: Inhaled large respiratory droplets from pathogens: Meningococcal meningitis, Pertussis (whooping cough), Mumps, and Influenza.
  • Required PPE & Protocol: Wear a standard fluid-resistant surgical mask upon entry or when within 1–2 meters; patient wears surgical mask during transport.

C. Airborne Precautions

Airborne isolation: Negative-pressure room and N95 particulate respirators
  • Indications: Pathogens transmitted via fine, lingering airborne nuclei: Active Pulmonary or Laryngeal Tuberculosis (TB), Measles (Rubeola), Chickenpox (Varicella).
  • Required Engineering & PPE: Airborne Infection Isolation Room (AIIR) with monitored negative air pressure (6–12 air changes/hour), air exhausted outside or HEPA-filtered, closed doors, and healthcare workers donning a fit-tested N95 or FFP2 particulate respirator.

7. Standard Infection Control Precautions (SICPs)

Your PDF emphasizes ten integrated occupational and clinical domains mandated across all healthcare encounters:

  1. Patient placement and infection-risk assessment
  2. Hand hygiene compliance
  3. Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette
  4. Personal protective equipment selection
  5. Safe decontamination of the healthcare care environment
  6. Safe reprocessing and sterilization of medical care equipment
  7. Safe handling and laundering of healthcare linen
  8. Safe containment and cleaning of blood and body fluid spillages
  9. Segregated clinical waste and sharps management
  10. Occupational safety and prophylaxis against percutaneous exposures

8. Institutional IPC Application & Facility Logistics

Your PDF outlines three essential operational policies to eliminate cross-contamination:

  • Separation of Clean and Contaminated Traffic: Physical workflow segregation ensuring used, contaminated surgical instruments and linen never intersect with sterile supplies.
  • Establishment of Clear Written Policies: Defining clinical accountability, daily disinfection schedules, and standard operating procedures.
  • Zoning & Movement Control: Physical demarcations (signage, red demarcation lines, closed isolation doors) restricting traffic flow of visitors, patients, and staff.
