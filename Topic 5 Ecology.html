🏠 TOPIC 5: Housing and Institutional Health

Housing Standards, Basic Needs, Disease & Accident Prevention, and Institutional Hygiene

💡 Core Principle: A house is not healthy simply because it has a roof and walls. It must provide safe surroundings, sufficient living space, clean indoor air, potable water, sanitary waste disposal, and protection from physical hazards.

This topic explores how the spaces where people live, study, work, and receive medical care can either protect health or expose inhabitants to disease and injury.

1. Meaning of Housing

According to the WHO Expert Committee definition quoted in your lecture, housing encompasses three integrated elements:

  • The physical residential structure used as shelter.
  • The environment and land surrounding that structure.
  • All necessary community services, facilities, equipment, and devices that support physical, mental, and social wellbeing.

Examples of residential structures: Caves, simple sheds, traditional huts or tukuls, tents, caravans, mobile vans, and modern residential buildings.

📌 Important Exam Point: Housing is far more than just the building itself—it incorporates the surrounding residential environment and essential municipal services.

2. Types of Housing: Key Classifications

Your PDF distinguishes between four critical concepts:

A. Standard Housing

A properly planned and constructed dwelling that is comfortable, secure, and preserves the physical, mental, and social wellbeing of its inhabitants.

Standard housing: Planned, safe, and fully serviced

B. Substandard Housing

A house that is poorly sited, planned, constructed, or maintained, and fundamentally lacks most basic sanitary principles of healthy housing.

Substandard housing: Structural deficiencies and lack of services

C. Slum

An entire residential neighborhood or urban sector dominated by substandard housing and frequently accompanied by severe overcrowding.

Slum: Area-wide concentration of substandard, overcrowded housing

D. Blighted Area

A tract of land that is fundamentally undesirable or unsuited for residential, agricultural, commercial, industrial, or recreational purposes (e.g., marshlands, steep rocky terrain, flood plains).

Blighted area: Inhospitable, rocky, or marshy terrain unsuitable for settlement
⚠️ Don't Confuse These Terms:
• Substandard housing: Refers to an individual deficient physical structure.
• Slum: Refers to an entire community or geographical area dominated by substandard dwellings and overcrowding.
• Blighted area: Refers to land intrinsically unsuited for development.
A single substandard house does not automatically classify the entire neighborhood as a slum!

3. Public Health Importance of Housing (5 WHO Principles)

Your PDF presents five foundational WHO principles connecting housing design to human health:

  1. Satisfaction of fundamental physiological needs.
  2. Satisfaction of fundamental psychological needs.
  3. Protection against communicable and noncommunicable diseases.
  4. Protection against accidents and physical injuries.
  5. Protection against excessive noise pollution.

4. Satisfaction of Physiological Needs

Physiological needs represent basic bodily requirements for normal metabolic and biological functioning.

A. Proper Thermal Environment

The dwelling must maintain comfortable thermal homeostasis to avoid excessive heat gain or loss.

  • Standard operative temperature: A minimum indoor temperature of 65°F (18.5°C) should be maintained in regularly occupied rooms for clothed, healthy adults at rest.
  • Vulnerable groups: A minimum of 70°F (21.3°C) measured at knee height is recommended for rooms occupied by young children and elderly individuals.
  • Hypothermia: Occurs when core body temperature drops below 95°F (35°C).

B. Adequate Ventilation

Facilitates continuous air exchange, diluting indoor moisture, odors, and respiratory aerosols while promoting thermoregulation.

  • Natural ventilation: Air exchange via operable doors and windows. The PDF guideline specifies that openable window area should equal 10–20% of floor area.
  • Artificial ventilation: Mechanical systems (fans, air conditioning).
  • Hyperthermia: Occurs when body core temperature rises above 104°F (40.32°C), manifesting as heat exhaustion or fatal heat stroke (which requires emergency medical cooling).

C. Reasonable Chemical Purity of Indoor Air

Indoor atmospheres must be free from toxic combustion products (e.g., carbon monoxide, particulate soot from biomass stoves, tobacco smoke).

D. Adequate Illumination

Natural and artificial illumination should allow visual performance without eye strain or excessive glare. PDF standards:

Location / ActivityMinimum Illumination Standard
Minimum natural illumination6 foot-candles
General habitable rooms6 foot-candles
Reading or close indoor workAt least 10 foot-candles
Stairs, hallways, and passagewaysAt least 1 foot-candle

Window area guideline: Window glass area should exceed 15% of floor area when interior walls and ceilings are finished with light-colored paint.

E. Direct Sunlight

Promotes endogenous vitamin D synthesis in skin and exerts a natural bactericidal/antimicrobial cleansing effect on indoor surfaces.

F. Exercise and Playground Space

Urban planning guidelines quoted in the PDF specify 3–5 acres of open park and playground space per 1,000 residents.

5. Satisfaction of Psychological Needs

A healthy house supports emotional, cognitive, and social wellbeing. Your PDF highlights seven specific psychological requirements:

  • Privacy: Essential space for personal solitude, dressing, and contemplation.
  • Normal Family Life: Adequate space fostering healthy familial bonds and care.
  • Normal Community Life: Integration with neighborhood social networks.
  • Work Performance: Conditions allowing study or domestic tasks without chronic fatigue.
  • Cleanliness: Facilities enabling effortless personal, laundry, and kitchen hygiene.
  • Aesthetic Satisfaction: Visually pleasing, orderly home design and landscape.
  • Concordance with Social Standards: Dwelling standards in harmony with prevailing local cultural norms.

Overcrowding: English Legislative Standard (Quoted in PDF)

Overcrowding leads to loss of privacy, chronic sleep deprivation, and accelerated airborne disease transmission.

Proper housing acts as a protective barrier against infectious diseases related to water, sanitation, vectors, and indoor air.

🦟 Applied Fly-Food Chain: Absence of a sanitary latrine + open uncollected garbage attracts houseflies. Flies mechanical-vector enteric pathogens onto uncovered food, triggering outbreaks of bacterial dysentery and typhoid fever within the household.

7. Protection Against Accidents and Injuries

Defective architectural design, poor lighting, and deferred maintenance contribute directly to domestic trauma.

Common Domestic Accidents:

  • Falls (stairs lacking handrails, slick untextured flooring).
  • Fires and burns (open hearth cooking, uninsulated electrical wires).
  • Accidental poisoning (storage of kerosene, pesticides, or medications within reach of children).
  • Structural collapse, electrocution, gas asphyxiation, and mechanical cuts.

High-Risk Demographics:

  • Infants and young toddlers
  • Elderly individuals (osteoporosis, impaired balance)
  • Residents of temporary/transitional shelters
  • Inhabitants of multiple-occupancy houses

8. Protection Against Excessive Noise

Noise is defined as unwanted, uncomfortable, or damaging sound. It is significantly more severe in dense urban environments.

Health Impacts of Chronic Noise Exposure:

  • Severe disruption of sleep cycles, chronic fatigue, and reduced worker productivity.
  • Permanent sensorineural hearing loss (exposures >85 dB).
  • Cardiovascular strain (hypertension, elevated resting heart rate).
  • Digestive neuroses, irritability, chronic psychological stress, and developmental delays in infants.

9. Institutional Health

An institution is an organized establishment founded for educational, social, medical, religious, or political purposes (e.g., schools, universities, hospitals, orphanages, prisons, military barracks).

Healthy institution: Planned layout, green space, accessibility, and sanitation

Fundamental Criteria of a Healthy Institution:

  • Site Selection: Stable, dry subsoil free from seasonal flooding.
  • Community Accessibility: Located within practical reach of the target population.
  • Isolation from Environmental Hazards: Safe setback from heavy traffic, industrial air emissions, and acoustic noise.
  • WASH Infrastructure: Ample potable water supplies, segregated sanitary flush toilets, and handwashing basins.
  • Vector & Rodent Control: Proofed building design preventing entry of vermin and insects.
  • Circulation & Fire Safety: Emergency egress routes, fire extinguishers, and clear perimeter access roads.
🏥 Clinical Hospital Scenario: A healthcare facility with deficient water supplies and improper medical waste segregation directly exposes patients, staff, and visitors to multidrug-resistant nosocomial (hospital-acquired) infections.
